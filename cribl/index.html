What TypeSafe’s Jev means for telemetry | Cribl

Cribl is evaluating TypeSafe’s Jev, a System One model designed for fast, cost-effective typed decision-making, relevant to high-volume telemetry use cases.

  • Jev skips sequential token generation common in LLMs, instead emitting calibrated decisions, which could be useful for routing, enrichment, and triage in telemetry pipelines.
  • Initial testing classifying logs into 28 types showed Jev’s accuracy lagged behind purpose-built classifiers and GPT-5.6 Terra, misclassifying events 2-3x more often, but it was 18x faster and 20x cheaper.
  • Jev excelled at LLM-as-judge tasks (evaluating AI agent responses) achieving >92% agreement with human judges at ~1% the cost.
  • Cribl believes System One models like Jev are most effective for bounded, high-volume decisions where intelligence isn't the limiting factor, but cost is – opening possibilities for applications like parser selection and PII detection.

Building a continuous security testing harness | Cribl

Building a Continuous Security Testing Harness

Cribl developed a continuous security testing harness leveraging agentic reasoning and deterministic validation to automate vulnerability research and improve recall.

  • The harness operates through five lanes: Intake (structuring reports/goals), Hunt (prioritizing testing areas), Validate (building and running proofs of concept locally), Cloud (validating in a dedicated environment), and Release (re-testing against new versions).
  • The system uses an agent for context-aware research (code analysis, trace building) and a deterministic runner to validate findings with controlled inputs, positive/negative controls, and evidence tied to unique nonces.
  • Coverage gaps (untested code, outdated reviews, known vulnerabilities) are used as inputs to prioritize research tasks, going beyond simple code changes as triggers.
  • A blind historical recall suite, using known vulnerabilities, is used to measure the system's effectiveness and identify areas for improvement in the research workflow.

GPT 6 Astra vs. GLM 5.3: Why performance scores aren’t the whole story | Cribl

Cribl’s SecIT Bench reveals that while GPT 6 Astra and GLM 5.3 achieve statistically similar performance scores (81.0% and 81.4% respectively), their approaches and resulting tradeoffs differ significantly.

  • Astra demonstrated greater token efficiency, completing investigations with 21 turns and 749,000 input tokens compared to GLM 5.3’s 45 turns and 2.2 million input tokens.
  • Despite Astra’s efficiency, GLM 5.3 was approximately 3.5x less expensive to run.
  • Astra was faster, averaging just over 8 minutes per trial vs. 14 minutes for GLM 5.3.
  • As dataset size increased, Astra’s performance held relatively consistent, while GLM 5.3’s accuracy declined.

How I think about detection in the pipeline | Cribl

  • Cribl argues that while detecting events in the pipeline is valuable for speed and cost-efficiency, it's insufficient for producing trustworthy alerts without correlation.
  • The piece highlights the importance of stateful detection logic (windows, thresholds, joins) which is often necessary for high-fidelity alerts, contrasting this with the stateless architecture common in high-throughput systems like those acquired by CrowdStrike (Onum).
  • Cribl advocates for a tiered architecture: in-pipeline processing for initial signal generation (parsing, enrichment, basic IOC matching) and a separate, persistent data store for correlation, backtesting, and investigation.
  • The author stresses the importance of customer control over the data layer, advocating for an architecture that avoids vendor lock-in and allows flexibility to change detection engines or search across multiple data stores using federated search.

How Cribl's security team solved their threat intelligence problem | Cribl

Cribl's security team developed an internal threat intelligence platform (TIP) built directly on top of Cribl Stream and Cribl Search to address challenges with operationalizing threat intelligence.

  • The solution leverages a STIX 2.1 knowledge graph to store and relate threat intelligence data, including threat actors, malware, and indicators of compromise (IOCs).
  • Intelligence feeds (e.g., abuse.ch, CISA KEV) and internal research are ingested, with a focus on curation workflow to prioritize relevant indicators and manage their lifecycle (freshness, expiration).
  • A “hunt workbench” enables enrichment of indicators with data from VirusTotal, Shodan, and GreyNoise, and directly launches KQL queries in Cribl Search for investigation.
  • Curated indicators are synced directly to Cribl lookup tables, immediately impacting real-time event enrichment within Cribl pipelines, eliminating the need for export/import processes and ensuring timely action based on threat intelligence.
